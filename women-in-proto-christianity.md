# Women in Proto-Christianity

To reconstruct 2nd century cultural literature context of Roman satire in Early Christian texts, it is useful to think about women in Proto-Christianity.

Proto-Christianity refers to a potential historical religious movement that already had some structure in place,
before the Jesus savior figure spread in popularity among them.
One of the observations made is that Early Christianity would not likely have spread as quickly as claimed by traditional dating from the 1st century.
New research suggests that Early Christianity is largely developed during the 2nd century CE,
which puts Proto-Christianity in the 1st century CE and maybe even in 1st century BCE.

There are several reasons why some scholar today believe that women played an important role in Proto-Christianity.
The most obvious one is that the majority of Early Christians were women.
However, due the historical bias of the traditional dating,
this has often been overlooked, as many scholars earlier thought the most important events happened before women could influence the development.
This bias is still present today, but mostly due to other controversies that get too much attention.

The following sections make a journey through the evidence and popular controversies,
explaining why the focus should be more toward women, what roles they might have that influenced Proto-Christianity,
and finally about the education of women and why these teachings appear in stories designed for children.

### 1. Centering Early Christianity in the 2nd century

In recent years, a througouh examination of the evidence about Early Christianity by scholars,
points toward center of events in the 2nd century and less so for the 1st century.

With other words, to explain the origin of Early Christianity, it is sufficient to start around early 2nd century or late 1st century.
If Jesus existed historically at all, then there is not much surviving details about the life of this person.

### 2. Simon Magus and John the Baptist

This project, The Century of Satire, attempts to reconstruct culture literature context of Roman satire in Early Christian texts.
After working for some time on this, a picture emerged that some possible historical Jesus probably did not know John the Baptist.
John the Baptist was likely added by ex-Simonians to point out Jesus as their savior figure,
precisely because Simon Magus was believed by Simonians to be a disciple of John the Baptist.

The result is with the little evidence left that might point back to some historical Jesus,
is a rebel that was crucified. Simon Magus was never described as crucified by Early Christians,
so the crucifixion of Jesus is more likely historical, assuming some historical Jesus existed, since it differs in belief between the two sects.
Scholars holding a Mythicist position argue that Jesus might have been not have been a historical crucifixion,
but taking place in a lower heaven, based on a literary tradition of midrash, where older texts are reinterpreted to make prophesies about the future.

### 3. The Ouroboros Hypothesis

Early Christianity itself therefore can best be explained with a literary origin around early 2nd century or late 1st century.
Most scholars believe that the Gospel of Mark is among the earliest texts.
Now, if one removes the section with John the Baptist from Mark, this naturally leads to The Ouroboros Hypothesis.
The ending of the original Mark points back to the beginning, where Jesus appears in Galilee.
The Ouroboros Hypothesis states that there is a text for children meant to be told in a book format,
where the main character climbs out of the last page and enters the first page.
This is Roman satire, written as a commentary on Hellenistic translation fables.

### 4. Human Trafficking

Another striking detail about Gospel of Mark, is that when Jesus gets arrested in a public park around 4 am,
he asks whether the arrest is due to the accusation of being a "leistes", which can mean a pirate.
A pirate is somebody who are illegally trafficking humans.
In the Acts of Thomas, Jesus sells his twin brother Thomas to a rich man in India as slave.
This is the only written document that any Early Christians claimed that Jesus wrote: A slave contract.
Judas' betrayal in Mark might therefore be a false accusation that Jesus did illegal human trafficking,
but not a denial that Jesus was a human trafficker.
The same theme of human trafficking appears in Philemon, when Paul sends back a slave, Onesimus, that had escaped.

### 5. Controversies

The two most controversial topics here, the debates about the historical Jesus and human trafficking,
tend to attract more attention.

Still, there is an elephant in the room, which has been there all the time, that not many people think about: Zoe.

### 6. Zoe and Ennoia

Early Christians worship Zoe, a goddess.
Zoe can be seen as a competitor to Ennoia, the Greek philosophical concept for Helen of Tyre in Simonianism.
The link here is very clear, as Zoe might be a similar concept for Mary of Magdala.
Notice that these two women are associated with two cities, Tyre and Magdala.
Both cities are located at geographical extremes to the west, in proximity to each other.
In Roman satire, this might be a literary device that refers to sexual climax,
which is how such texts were used in mystery cults.

Today, there is no evidence that directly explains where Zoe comes from.
However, Helen of Tyre was believed by Simonians to be a reincarnated goddess,
who previously lived as Helen of Troy.
The obvious literature context here, is to use Hellenistic texts to create prophecies about Jesus.
If a similar process was used to write about Zoe, then the King Midas myth is a possible alternative,
where Zoe is the daughter of Midas that was turned into a golden statue.
Midas baptizes Zoe in a river in Turkey, on the order of Dionysus, to bring Zoe back to life.

### 7. Dionysus, Isis and Medea

Early Christianity was part of the mystery cults, where Dionysus was very popular.
Other deities that are popular in mystery cults are Isis and Medea.
Isis is the mother of Horus and widow of Orisis.
Medea is the granddaughter of Helios, the Sun god and priestess of Hecate, the witch goddess.

While Zoe might be direct competitor to Ennoia in Simonianism,
Ennoia in turn might be competing with Isis and Meda.
Therefore, even Zoe might not have been seen as competing directly with Isis and Medea by Early Christians,
Zoe was probably at least competing indirectly with Isis and Medea, by being influenced by Ennoia.

### 8. Yahwism

Scholarship in recent years has also developed the idea of Yahwism.
In Yahwism, the deity Yahweh is the head deity of a pantheon, but the pantheon might itself not be the same as polytheism found in the bible.
The pantheon could vary from place to place.
The Greek name for Yahweh would be Zeus,
and since Dionysus means "son of Zeus",
people who used Greek in Samaria and Judea would naturally think of Jesus and Dionysus as parallel figures.
Near Nazareth, the location it is believed to have today, which might not be a historical city with that name,
there is a temple for Dionysus.
This temple might be a source of inspiration to 2nd century Early Christians,
for the story about Jesus growing up in Nazareth.

### 9. Literary breakthrough

Now, with these pieces of the puzzle brought into your attention,
it might be easier to see that the Jesus savior figure in particular is not that central when thinking about Proto-Christianity.
With other words, Proto-Christianity in the 1st century might have prophecies about some savior figure,
based on some possible historical Jesus that was a rebel and crucified,
but this mystery cult was not that popular,
before it got a literary breakthrough around early 2nd century or late 1st century.
The literary breakthrough was the event that made the Jesus savior figure popular, but not vice versa.
Jesus was not popular in that sense, but that does not mean that there was no such thing as Proto-Christianity.
On the contrary, Zoe worship probably existed in the 1st century, due to the influence of Sibyl oracles.

### 10. The Sibyl oracles

The Sibyl oracles were popular and seen as important among Proto-Christians.
These were women that prophesized about the future.
When later Christian writers defend Christianity, they use sayings of Sibyl oracles.
Also, when Early Christians are criticized, they were accused of plagiarizing the Sibyls.
Regardless of which side in this debate people argued, the Sibyl oracles were claimed as spiritual authority.
In Sibyl oracle sayings, Zoe is used as a theological concept in prophecy.

### 11. Personification of Zoe as a goddess

There is evidence that Zoe was personified as a goddess by Early Christians during the 2nd century.
When put together with the evidence that Early Christianity can be seen as a largely 2nd century development,
the archeological evidence of Dionysus worship in Samaria and Judea,
plus the popularity of Sibyl oracles,
and finally Zoe as believed to be a historical person as daughter of King Midas,
this suggests overall that Zoe might have been a personified goddess already in the 1st century.

### 12. The King Midas myth

The King Midas myth is one of older classical stories, alongside the Homeric myths.
When seeking out myths to support arguments about prophecy, the vast majority based their tradition on the Homeric myths.
However, when moving outside Homeric myths, there is not that much surviving texts available.
The King Midas myth is one of the few stories that survived, but in a fragmented state.
The best link between Zoe in the King Midas myth and Zoe in Early Christianity, is through a similar practice in Simonianism.

### 13. Polytheism in Early Christianity

Many Early Christians were polytheists.
Polytheism in ancient religion is frequently misunderstood.
Some people think about Henotheism in relation to the Old Testament,
where Henotheism is defined as the belief in the existence of many deities,
but worship of only one.
However, angles in the Old Testament are frequently described as sons of Yahweh.
When you put angles into the picture, this becomes clear that Yahweh is the head of a pantheon,
where angles can be thought of as smaller deities.
The conflicts in the Old Testament are primarily about ownership of land,
not against Ba'al or Asherah in general,
but because these deities were worshiped in the land they wanted to conquer with help of Yahweh, the warrior deity.
The Old Testament did not even exist in the later form after the collapse of Bronze Age civilizations,
but was partially used around the period of the Dead Sea scrolls.
The earliest surviving records of anyone writing about Moses in length,
using that name and in the context of the familiar story,
was from works of Philo of Alexandria, a 1st century author.

### 14. Proto-Christianity

Therefore, it becomes impossible to say with certainty,
what Proto-Christianity in the 1st century might have been like.
Too much attention is put toward the Jesus savior figure,
while the evidence suggest very little of it goes back to the 1st century.
To go back to Proto-Christianity in the 1st century, it is therefore reasonable to use another approach.

### 15. Women in Proto-Christianity

What if, instead of focusing on a single possible historical male figure,
the life of women in Proto-Christianity takes the center stage?
There is a tendency that most scholarship focuses too much on men,
while women are ignored in general.

### 16. "She" - some historical woman

It is difficult to talk about women in general,
so one can use a "she" to refer to some woman,
without elaborating in detail which woman is referred to.
This technique is used to highlight that "she" is some historical or mythical person,
not a group of women, or femininity in general, under Proto-Christianity.

### 17. The Oracle

"She" could have been an oracle.

Oracles were women, that were popular and seen as authorative.
These women were associated with prophecy in general,
for example when Early Christians are accused of plagiarizing the Sibyl oracles.

### 18. The Scripture

"She" could have been a personification of scripture.

Sophia was a personified Greek goddess of Wisdom.
Some biblical scholars argue that Wisdom in some Old Testament texts was originally a goddess,
that was rewritten as a theological concept of the scripture itself.
Therefore, it was a later editorial work to remove the goddess from worship.

This is one way that a personified goddess, Zoe, might have been removed.

### 19. Zoe's Tree

"She" could have been the divine supervisor of the tree of immortality.

In the King Midas myth, Zoe discovers that her tree in the garden has been turned into gold.
Zoe starts crying and runs to Midas, embracing him.
The moment Zoe touches Midas, she turns into a golden statue.

You can imagine Zoe as this golden statue, frozen in a posture of distress and seeking comfort at the same time.
This can be a powerful metaphor for women living in abusive relationships,
where they are trapped in pain, both emotionally and physically, and by the desire to please.
Instead of being seen as a real person, she becomes property to be owned, measured in gold by her value.

The morality is that by treating a woman as a slave, a life is lost in the process.
To ressurect her back to from this inanimate state, she must be given the water of life.
This comes from the observation that humans need water to live.

The tree, which hibernates each winter, is brought back to life through the power of Dionysus.
This power is transmitted through the water of life.
Just like humans need water to live, so does trees.

Zoe's tree, of Tree of Life, is not a metaphor for all living beings.
It is a metaphor for the power of immortality, that brings something back to life.
Resurrection is a demonstration of this power.
Immortality can not be demonstrated on something that is alive, but only on something that has been dead.
This is why the tree has this symbolism, of something dead coming back to life,
as a visible sign of the spring.

When Zoe is baptized by Midas, she is brought back to life,
coming back from a state of distress and unsatified need for comfort.

### 20. The Warrior

"She" could have been a warrior.

When the Romans stated to integrate religious rituals in Samaria with Hellenism in the 1st century,
some people in Samaria, who felt disatisfied with this development, might have moved to Judea.
They might use Judea as their breeding ground for rebellion.
These rebels might have developed older religious rituals for their own purposes.
Roman authorities probably underestimated the ability of Judea itself to wage war,
but were scared of the rebellion spreading over to Samaria.

Archeological evidence suggests that there were religious washing rituals,
related to Apocalypticism, based on official temple practices.
The idea is that to enter the temple in Jerusalem, one must satisfy certain rules of cleanliness.
Failing to satisfy these rules of cleanliness was seen as risking being struck down by Yahweh.
As people thought the end of times were coming,
they believed that Yahweh would make the whole earth his temple ground,
and anyone that were not ready in that moment would die.

This form of Apocalypticism is kind of like an alarm bell of a coming war.
Religious washing rituals are not mere superstition, but a way to recruit soldiers.

Baptizement of women in this historical context could be to recruit women as soldiers,
often as archers who trained to shoot with bow and arrow.

If the historical Jesus was a rebel leader,
then it makes sense that women that followed him were warriors.
This could explain why Early Christians later talk about Logos and Zoe.

### 21. The Holy Spirit

"She" could have been the personification of a commune spirit.

The Holy Spirit is closely associated with the ritual of baptizement in Early Christianity.
This ritual was performed in adult age, either through conversion, or as a coming of age ritual.
In this sense, baptizement functions very similar to initation in mystery cults.
Through initiation, the initiated is seen as accessing the commune spirit.
They become a member of the mystery cult and loyal to its teachings.

The Holy Spirit is not only a divine aspect, but also symbolizes the followers themselves.
Most scholars believe that the majority of Early Christians were women.
Therefore, it is natural to assume that in Proto-Christianity in the 1st century,
most followers there were women too.
When somebody used the phrase "The Holy Spirit" in conversations,
the image that would come to mind, might be naturally a woman.

### 22. The Spy

"She" could have been a spy.

The first spy network in Judea was established under Herod the Great.
Very little is known about this spy network,
but it is natural to think about the possibility of using women as spies.

One ability that is useful for women who serve as spies,
is the ability to read and write.
There are some Early Christian texts that likely were written by women.

Using Zoe from the King Midas myth as a religious ritual for baptizement,
could be motivated to trick Romans into thinking this was a Hellenistic ritual.
This might explain why baptizement later was replaced with John the Baptist,
without any further elaboration of why a such ritual was important.
The original purpose of the ritual might have been a secret.

### 23. Education for women

"She" could have been a teacher and student.

One possible historical scenario that could have kicked off Early Christianity,
is that Roman authorities wanted to de-escalate political tensions in Samaria and Judea.
They wanted to attract talent from these regions to their cities in Antioch, Rome and Alexandria.

Since kidnapping and trafficking of women as sex slaves had been going on for a long time,
the prospect of a young man moving to a big city in the early 2nd century, without a lot of families to marry into,
while seeing women from their own culture hanging on the street corners selling their own bodies,
might discourage them from moving.

As a result, the Roman government might have opened up education for these women from Samaria and Judea,
to were prostitutes living in their big cities.
It solved two problems in one move: Get rid of the sight of these women at the street corners,
and attracting talent from these regions where they feared an uprising rebellion.
The goal was to educate these women, at least to an extent where they could pass off in marriage as women of higher status.
Little did the Romans know that this program would have little effect on de-escalate political tensions in Judea.
Even more, that the women that gained access to education would end up changing the world.

A such education program would explain why Early Christians use the phrase prostitution about the background of their converts.
Frequently, this is interpeted as a metaphorical prostitution,
but upon closer examination, there are multiple stories that point to a similar theme.

For example, the story of the woman by the well that met Jesus, in the Gospel of John.
This woman had prior sexual relationships, yet she was offered the water of life.

Another parallel is that Helen of Tyre in Simonianism,
which competed with Mary of Magdala,
was believed by Simonians to have been a sex slave in Tyre.
Jesus even goes to Tyre in some stories and talk to women there.

### 24. Stories for children

When educating women, one important thing is to give them stories that they can tell their own children.
This was one of the reasons why educated women were of higher status and seen as attractive.
Raising children well is part of the planning when starting a family.

Now, there is this common bias, that when people think of children under Early Christianity,
that one thinks mostly about boys, while girls are merely given an afterthought.
Many women died under childbirth in the ancient world,
so young girls often had to take over the leadership of the house.
This meant, that stories for children had to help girls grow up fast.
Boys were often given more later opportunities to learn about e.g. Homeric myths.

It is therefore not that strange, that the gospels about Jesus have so much to do with women.
If Early Christianity starts with children stories, then it would not be uncommon to orient them toward young girls growing up.
They would need to learn how to sort out complex moral issues,
just by living in a violent environment, where girls often became victims of human trafficking.

### 25. Male main characters in stories for women

Women served important functions in the first gospels, of witnessing execution and visiting the tomb.
This is accepted by almost all scholars, that the stories were men play a greater role in these events,
were later editions, that changed these important functions of women in the earlier versions.

It is rare that women are depicated in writing as the main character of their own story in this time period,
contrasted with other places in other time periods, for example in the Sumerian civilization, where the goddess Inanna was extremely popular.
However, while women are rarely the main character, they can challenge male authority in some stories.
This happens in several Early Christian texts, signifying that these texts were centered around women.

In some ways, one can think of the Jesus character as being added to the story,
to serve as a challenge and support for female characters.
Jesus is kind of an divine objectification of the male gender, seen through the lens of Early Christian women,
that possess powers of immortality, life ressurected from death, representing Zoe.
One might even go so far as saying that Jesus, the Logos, is there to allow room to talk about Zoe.

The people who think that Zoe might have been merely an afterthought developed in Early Christianity,
might gotten their history upside down.
Proto-Christianity might have primarly developed around women,
where the need for a male savior figure was more or less a cosmetic literary justification to continue this tradition.
This explains why so little of Jesus life can be traced back to the 1st century.
Maybe Jesus was just added to a religion, that was mainly practiced by women.
