# Why Jesus probably did not know John the Baptist

To reconstruct 2nd century cultural literature context of Roman satire in Early Christian texts,
it is useful to consider the hypothesis that Jesus did not know John the Baptist.

Biblical scholarship in recent years, e.g. by Markus Vinzent,
suggests that what is known about the historical Jesus is very little,
far less than was earlier assumed.
Markus Vinzent focuses his scholarship on the Marcion collection.

The following argument is not based directly  on the research by Markus Vinzent,
but on the bigger picture that seems to emerge, of a possible future direction of scholarship.

What Vinzent' research changes, is to focus the attention toward the varied Early Christian writings and their production through various schools in the 2nd century.

The argument here is by first presenting 4 independent lines of evidence that suggest the sections in the gospels with John the Baptist were written in the 2nd century:

1. [Other sources than Josephus are needed for texts to be written before the 2nd century](#1-other-sources-than-josephus-are-needed-for-texts-to-be-writen-before-the-2nd-century)
2. [Rhetorical training in the 2nd century](#2-rhetorical-training-in-the-2nd-century)
3. [Theodotion and quotations of the Hebrew bible](#3-theodotion-and-quotations-of-the-hebrew-bible)
4. [John the Baptist was the master of Simon Magus](#4-john-the-baptist-was-the-master-of-simon-magus)

The final part of the argument is the conclusion, that since there is no evidence of sections of texts about John the Baptist in connection to Jesus Christ,
dating before the 2nd century, it follows that Jesus probably did not know John the Baptist.

[Conclusion: Jesus probably did not know John the Baptist](#conclusion-jesus-probably-did-not-know-john-the-baptist)

### 1. Other sources than Josephus are needed for texts to be writen before the 2nd century

There is only one historian that records anything about John the Baptist: Josephus at the end of the 1st century.

This means, anyone who writes about John the Baptist, uses other sources than Josephus,
or they write most likely in the 2nd century.

To claim that some Early Christian text is written before the 2nd century,
requires presenting evidence that it uses other sources than Josephus.

This part is important to understand the rest of the argument.
Basically, without evidence that some Early Christian writer uses sources about John the Baptist outside Josephus,
there is no reason to date such sections earlier than the 2nd century.
Unless of course, such dating is supported by some other line of evidence.

### 2. Rhetorical training in the 2nd century

Rhetorical training in the 2nd century consists of taking historical texts and rearrange the events to create something new.
This is a possible way that some of the Early Christian texts were produced.
Hence, just because somebody writes about John the Baptist in other settings than Josephus,
is not evidence they use other sources than Josephus.

### 3. Theodotion and quotations of the Hebrew bible

Around mid 2nd century, a translation by Theodotion of the Hebrew bible into Greek becomes the most popular among Early Christian writers.
Quotes that probably uses this translation are more likely to date from the mid 2nd century and later.

The sections with John the Baptist contains such quotes, that might be more common after Theodotion's translation was published.
Hence, such sections are more likely to date from the 2nd century, not the 1st century.

### 4. John the Baptist was the master of Simon Magus

Some Early Christian writings claim that John the Baptist was the master of Simon Magus.

Since Jesus Christ was a competitor savior figure to Simon Magus in the 2nd century,
it is likely that followers of Jesus Christ wanted to present Jesus Christ as better than Simon Magus.

In the sections where John the Baptist interacts with Jesus in the gospels,
John the Baptist is described as humbling himself in front of Jesus.
Now, consider that for those who believed that Simon Magus was a disciple of John the Baptist,
these sections would be provocative, since they indirectly claim Jesus Christ is better than Simon Magus.

The logic in this argument is that since John the Baptist was the master of Simon Magus,
then if John the Baptist humbled himself in front of Jesus,
then Jesus Christ was a better savior figure than Simon Magus.

Notice that a such argument by Early Christians would be difficult for followers of Simon Magus to attack.
Since there is no mention of Simon Magus explicitly, such attacks might be defended by counter-accusations of reading too much into the texts.

While a such argument would be cleverly designed, this is not the main point here.

The main point here is that such an argument would only work after the Simon Magus savior figure
was established in the 2nd century, and competing with Jesus Christ.
Meaning, these sections must likely have been added in the 2nd century.

### Conclusion: Jesus probably did not know John the Baptist

History is a science of what is more probable.
When we find that our earlier assumptions about history can be questioned due to lack of evidence,
it is a good idea to simply stop making such assumptions if they are not backed by evidence.

When all the sections that include John the Baptist in the gospels seems to date from the 2nd century,
it means that John the Baptist might have been added by Early Christians, to compete with Simon Magus in Simonianism.

If John the Baptist was simply added to their stories, and there is no other evidence that Jesus knew John the Baptist,
then there is no reason to believe that Jesus knew John the Baptist while he lived.

Early Christian writers even claim John the Baptist was a close relative to Jesus Christ,
which might be a sign that they are overly eager to prove the historicity of their savior figure.
Such eagerness might not be evidence of them honestly retelling the historical life of Jesus,
because a defending argument of something is often a reaction to accusations of lying.
In turn, such accusations of lying, are also more likely to happen when they are simply lying.
Therefore, eagerness to defend the the historicity of the Jesus Christ savior figure in such a peculiar way by including John the Baptist,
is not evidence of an accurate record of Jesus' life, but evidence of a section written in the 2nd century.
