# Ida - The Wise Woman by the Well

To reconstruct 2nd century cultural literature context of Roman satire in Early Christian texts,
it is useful to consider the story where Jesus meets a woman by a well as originating in a satirical play for children.

In mystery cults, there is a cultural tradition of performing satire for children,
while the grown ups are going through the rituals to get initiated.
One hypothesis is that Jesus is a character being used in such satire,
that becomes popular over time and results in a new mystery cult using Jesus as a savior figure.

The idea is that there is a wise woman, Ida (Greek: "Οἶδα", meaning "I saw it") that is meeting Jesus by the well in The Gospel of John.
They engage in a battle of wits, using the cultural background of men meeting women by the well to find a wife.
Men were unwelcome by the well at the time, unless they could save face by flirting or by bringing some young and attractive men with them.
Since Jesus does not have his students/disciples around, he needs to improvise, but he is too old and he faces the most strict woman of them all: The wise woman.
Author's intention might have to include this section from a play to break away from Jesus as secret Messiah in previous gospels,
where Jesus' role as Messiah is spread as a rumor in the city as a distraction, without Ida realizing Jesus is the actual Messiah.

### 1. Roman satire in mystery religions: Dirty jokes for children in the ancient world

Mandy Moorehol is a satirist interested in Early Christianity that suggests that the New Testament is Roman satire.
You can watch his interview with Jacob Berman [here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LT-a9KeEdkw) sharing some of his ideas.

A common problem with satire is that people can read the texts, without realizing that it is satire.
Many people have taken PhDs using some text as a thesis and passed review,
before people discovered later that the text was originally satire.

This project (The Century of Satire) is not aiming at interpreting specific texts in the New Testament as satire,
but to reconstruct some the historical literature context, such that people might be able to reason about why some Early Christians
seemingly write in the genre of Roman satire.
In this process, the goal is to be able to use Joker Calculus to classify the language bias of some texts.
There might be multiple language biases that fits the same text,
but by using expressions in Joker Calculus, it will be easier to tell those language biases apart.
While this goal overlaps with Moorehol's project, it is important to distinguish some possible origin as satire
and the steps in the evolution of Early Christianity where people change language bias.
It is not sufficient to just reconstruct the origin, but also to explain how the interpretation is likely to have evolved over time.
This is needed to better understand the entire literature body of Early Christian texts and not just the New Testament,
which makes up a small part of the texts in Early Christianity.
With other words, the specific interpretation that survived of the New Testament or whether it originated as satire,
is not that much of historical interest.
There are other texts that are much more difficult to explain unless one interprets them through a lens of Roman satire.

For example, in the Gospel of Andrew, there is a boy that sneaks into women's bath.
Just thinking why such a thing is written about, is very weird, in something that might be read as a holy text,
unless one compares it with e.g. Homer's epic where Achilles hides, clothed as a girl and lives some time among girls.
Homer's epic was holy text for a lot of people in the ancient Hellenized world.
However, Homer's epic is not Roman satire.
While this event with Achilles has comedy value, it is not the central theme of the story.
Roman satire is a literature genre where one is repeatedly making jokes, which seems to be the case in the Gospel of Andrew.

The plays that were performed for children in mystery cults might have contained lots of dirty jokes,
since the taboo against sexual language was not as much present in Early Christianity as it became later.
This was probably because Early Christians did not use a cannon, except followers of Marcion of Sinope.
When they refer to some cannon of texts, it is Marcion's and while Marcion was condemned as a heretic later,
he was not during his life being attacked that way.
It was more like out of jealousy or ambition that people sought to overcome his approach to Early Christianity.
So, when people use dirty jokes in texts, it is not necessarily that people see these texts as cannon to be protected.
Neither does it help that the cannon that is found in most versions of the bible was heavily redacted and forged.
This means, the taboo against sexual language is a cultural phenomena of Late Christians not handling criticism very well.
After all, they were engaged in bloody wars against all religions they saw as competitors to their own.

Early Christians use the Socratic dialogue in texts to establish arguments, similar to the way Plato wrote.
However, in Roman satire, the analogue of the Socratic dialogue might take the form of a battle of wits.
It is a style that makes fun of the Socratic dialogue and often used to bring the audience closer to the characters.
This can be combined with a play-act where you do not see the entire play in terms of the dialogue only,
but in addition one requires body language and perhaps some scene props to provide context.

### 3. Reconstructed play with possible body language of actors and cultural references

This reconstructed play is not following the text in a rigid fashion,
because some of it, particularly at the end, is theological speech that would be boring in a play for children.
I take a lot of creative freedom here, in attempt to reconstruct the feeling that people might get from a such play.

1. The disciples/students are "liberated" into the city, having fun and purchasing food in the market place
2. Jesus has no backup when going to the well (no students to sway the women this time)
3. Jesus is asking a woman for water, feeling a bit out of the game (men at the time do not go to the well,
unless they are looking for a wife, so to save face, you have to at least be good at flirting or this would be a great way to get some enemies among the women)
4. The woman says "No, you're a Jew and we don't like you people" (when people are traveling by,
they are used to make them begging on their knees and promise something in return, by either offering money or doing some part time work)
5. Jesus (cursing himself silently for not bringing any students) tries another approach:
"I would give you living water if you asked me" (Jesus knows this joke is lame, but he is going to try anyway, since the thought of his students seeing him doing laundry is unbearable)
6. The woman, hearing that joke for the hundredth time, sighs, crosses her arms and says
"Don't mess with me, it will be very hard for you to get any water without equipment" (For the uninitiated: It's not the equipment in the well she is referring to).
Wondering who this guy is which is too old for this game.
Ah, a wanna-be patriach? She cheeks up "Who do you think you are?
This water is ours and you are not greater than our ancestor that built this well" (she says this while judging Jesus' body build and posture:
It seems he has been too much inside reading and she could easily beat him in a fight)
7. Jesus, seeing the joke was not well received, tries a follow up: "People who drink this water gets thirsty again,
but the water I give, will become a well of life that last forever, longer than a century, even"
8. The woman eye-rolls, says "OK, give me this water so I don't have to go here anymore"
(they are both just acting out roles of a satirical play for children that everyone learned when growing up in this Early Christian mystery cult,
so this joke exchange might be something most people were already familiar with)
9. Jesus realizes this is not working, gives up and says "I want to speak to your boss. Where is your husband?"
10. The woman says "I'm in charge here. I don't have a husband"
(If you are unlucky, then you meet the wise woman who is the highest authority on water usage and the strictest of them all.
When introducing herself, the wise woman can't say "I am the wise woman here", because that would be totally lame among these hardworking women,
but using a cultural reference joke that they don't have a husband, which might seem weird for people not participating in the culture)
11. Jesus sees an opening and says "What you're saying is correct: You had 5 husbands and yet,
the man you have now is not enough man for you" winks at the woman
(wise women were often married for political reasons and this leads to a whole genre of jokes about husbands of wise women)
12. The woman relaxes by this joke and starts liking Jesus. She says "Yup, that's a prophet right there.
Btw, my name is Ida" (It means that Jesus won the battle of wits.
Ida here in Greek: "Οἶδα" means "I saw it" that could be a play on Mount Ida in Homer's epic about the Troy war versus battle of wits.
The mythical mount Ida (Greek: "Ἴδη") is the place where Paris, the man that kidnaps Helen and causes the war, grows up as an orphan)
13. The woman says "I'm gonna leave this equipment unattended for a moment while you do your business.
In the meantime, I'll go to the city causing a distraction.
If they hear I've given water to a Jew without getting something in return, they will never let me live this down.
Don't let anyone you don't trust on your life near the well, or you'll regret it"
14. Jesus, a bit nervous from the threat, does thumbs up
15. The woman goes to the city and yells out loudly "A WEIRD MAN BY THE WELL THINKS HE IS MESSIAH"
16. Jesus whistling a shepherd's melody to himself, while lowering the bucket into the well,
hears the ruckus from distance, turns his head toward the city in slight confusion.
He raises a finger into the air as if he is trying to remember something he forgot, then nods to himself: "Oh yeah, I forgot to tell her that was supposed to be a secret..."

(AUDIENCE LAUGHS, END OF ACT)

### 4. Ida - in Greek "Οἶδα" - as a possible name of the wise woman

In the Gospel of John, Jesus declares that he is Messiah to the woman he meets by the well.
However, right before that point, the woman tells she believes Messiah is coming.

John 4:24
```
The woman said to Him, “I know that Messiah is coming” (who is called Christ).
“When He comes, He will tell us all things.”

λέγει αὐτῷ ἡ γυνή Οἶδα ὅτι Μεσσίας ἔρχεται, ὁ λεγόμενος Χριστός: ὅταν ἔλθῃ ἐκεῖνος, ἀναγγελεῖ ἡμῖν ἅπαντα.
[says] [to him/her] [or] [woman] [Oída, I saw it] [that] [Messiah] [is coming],
[the] [so-called] [Christ]: [when] [come] [that], [announces] [us] [everything].
```

The word "Oída" does not appear earlier in the text and is next to the word "γυνή" (woman) that appears for the first time when Jesus meets her.

It is a huge stretch (but since this is just speculation anyway),
this could be the name of the woman that was translated "I saw it" or "I know that" later on.

I want to point out that in previous dialogues in this text, we learn the name of the person that Jesus talks to.
It is very curious that when it comes to this woman by the well, she is not mentioned by name.

One possibility is that the name of the woman was Mary of Magdala and this is how Jesus meets her for the first time.
The name might have been erased to detach Mary of Magdala from the use of Zoe of the beginning of the text,
because in that sense she is a reincarnated goddess parallel to Helen of Tyre in Simonianism,
that became problematic in Late Christianity since most people were born into the religion and did not need a mystical character to represent Eastern religions
or places somewhere-nearby-but-not-too-far.
It became too unpractical to move Mary of Magdala from a Jewish woman, to Greek, or somewhere else that became too far for the story to be believable.
Instead, they settled on three wise men from the East showing up at the birth of Jesus, so that Mary of Magdala could remain a Jewish woman.

Another possibiltity is that the woman was named Ida or something like it, a different woman than Mary of Magdala,
which role was to blurt out the secret that Jesus was Messiah to use this as part of the story.
In the oldest gospel, Mark, Jesus never says explicitly that he is Messiah, but people ask each other this question about him.
This could be because at the time when Mark is written, the Jesus savior figure is not well known as a savior, but perhaps instead as a cynic philosopher slave.
Mary of Magdala, using this approach, might have been a priestess that annoints Jesus, that later was replaced by John the Baptist.
Or, Mary of Magdala could have been a goddess that splits into two roles over time, Mary Jesus' mother and Mary of Magdala.

If the woman that meets Jesus by the well had a name,
then it might not have been intentional to erase her name,
or it could also have been intentional to try make it seem possible
that Mary of Magdala was indeed the woman by the well by some Early Christians that want to tie her to Zoe,
and closer to Helen of Tyre in Simonianism,
while saying this explicitly so in the text, would be too controversial at the time.
As strange as this sounds, this would not be unusual.
The gospels are full of ambiguities that allow multiple interpretations,
possibly as a way to gain more followers by letting them use their own imagination.

### 5. Misogynistic interpretation in Late Christianity

As Early Christianity evolves, the texts are redacted or reinterpreted with increasinly mysogynistic views.
This might have contributed to some female characters missing their names over time.

In the case of the woman by the well, it is incredible that the strong cultural norms and complex social and politics around wells,
did not actually come up earlier as a way for interpreting the text.
Men in general were not welcome around wells, because women had to look out for kids while doing the hard work of bringing water to the surface. 
This activity supported the whole existence of the village and without it, there could be no life.
The well itself is a ancient machine that symbolizes in the landscape how difficult it is to get access to water.

The way most Christians read this section today, makes the woman by the well appear very dull and submissive.
For example, that the problem was Jesus asking her for water by talking to her, a Samaritan woman,
instead of seeing her as a protector of the well and water usage in the village,
which simply refuses Jesus because they are not on friendly terms with the Jews and sharing water is only allowed with allies.
Alternatively, that she wants Jesus as a stranger to offer some money or a service in return for the water.
The latter would be more common social behavior, since when Jesus is entering the area around the well,
he is intruding on a mostly private space for women in the village.

It is kind of like a man entering a woman's bathroom in the airport and requiring service from a female flight attendant,
that happens to be in that bathroom at the same time as the man.
Try imagine how the flight attendant would react naturally.
The flight attendant is still a flight attendant in this situation,
but she is not denying the man a service simply because she does not like him or something he did onboard the plane,
but because the man is intruding in her private space that she protects.
Which is a completely different way of looking at the situation.

Perhaps it is because people get used to interpreting Jesus as a character without flaws or making errors,
so when he intrudes in the private space of woman this aggressive behavior must somehow have a theological explanation.
This is in contrast with the idea of divinity among Early Christians, where deities can make human mistakes.
The deities in Early Christianity are not omniscient, omnipotent nor omnipresent.
They might have magical abilities, but they think much more like humans do than later, when theologians want to unify
