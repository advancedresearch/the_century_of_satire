# The Historical Jesus

To reconstruct 2nd century cultural literature context of Roman satire in Early Christian texts,
it is useful to consider hypotheses about who the historical Jesus was and whether he even existed.

In Christianity, it is generally believed that Jesus was a being with supernatural powers and that the gospels in the New Testament described historical events about Jesus' life.

Under the assumption of naturalism, which is a philosophical belief that only natural laws and forces operate in the universe,
the historical Jesus, if he even existed, would not have any supernatural powers, unless he possessed technology that would seem like supernatural powers to ancient people.

Historians add further assumptions, due to lack of evidence of people with advanced technology in the ancient world,
that the historical Jesus must have lived a life like any ordinary human: Getting born, live aging 1 year per calendar year and die,
without any resurrection or e.g. raising people from death, feeding thousands with little bread etc.
With other words, the miracles Jesus are known for, are logically inconsistent with the assumptions that historians add to the position of naturalism.

In comparison, when it comes to healing people, modern science cures millions of people year systematically for diagnoses that would be fatal in the ancient world.
With other words, in a single year, modern science saves more lives than claimed in all the holy texts of all ancient religions, combined.
Rationally, the supernatural claims of healing in ancient texts, are not impressive from the perspective of consequence ethics.
These aspects are simply not worth any worship of modern humans, because if they are, modern humans would worship modern science instead.

The healing mircales of Jesus can be put in context of medicinal advancements during the 2nd century, particularly in surgery.
This advancement produced over 200 different systematic methods, many which were life saving.
Therefore, it is not unlikely that healing miracles were seen as visions of future medicinal progress,
by expecting that the rate of improvements in medicine knowledge would continue.

Modern science confirms that if ancient people continued the advancements that happened in the 2nd century over time,
then it would be possible to achieve healing on a large scale.
So, the people who had visions of further medicinal progress during the 2nd century were basically right,
even though they might be wrong about the details.

Early Christians during the 2nd century believe that knowledge was important for salvation.
This makes it difficult to say exactly what they mean by e.g. Jesus walking on water.
The expression "Son of God" could have supernatural semantics, but it might also be used about somebody very talented or skilled person.

On top of this ambiguity, the gospels were used as advertisement for schools of medicine,
where people learned surgery, anatomy and physiology from cutting up animals systematically.
Philosophy as we know it today, was a mental training that students went through to learn how to become a doctor.

For example, Aristotle was not seen mainly as an abstract thinker, but as zoologist: A person who studied animals.
Zoos and botanic gardens were built in the ancient world to educate students about nature across the known world.

Basically, everything you think of as philosophy today, is just small fraction of what it meant to be a philosopher in the ancient world.
Cutting up animals systematically as part of the training, was much more important than memorizing philosophical dialogues.
The basic reason is that if you can not perform surgery by following instructions, then you can not save people and your knowledge would be useless.
Unless you excelled in some field, e.g. mathematics, you had to be a generalist, who learned a lot about many subjects, many which included practical knowledge.

Christianity for the major part of history has been about a monopoly on healthcare, backed by the state.
In this system, the practice of medicine was based on pagan religion, using Galen's works for over 1300 years.
The accusations of witchcraft or performing magic, was not actually because it was seen as demonic by doctors and priests.
It was simply a matter of economics, where any opposition against the official monopoly on healthcare was punishable by death.

With other words, most likely, everything people taught you about how Christianity worked in modern times, is a lie based on modern myths.

This background knowledge is important to establish, before we explore the historical Jesus.
The reason is that how Christianity functioned for many centuries, is a result of the development in the 2nd century, not the 1st century.
The historical Jesus goes back to the 1st century, but we have very little scientific evidence and have to use multiple hypotheses.

### Removing the layers of medicine: Jesus Christ vs Simon Magus

In Rome around the mid 2nd century, there are two schools who compete with each other,
one that has Jesus Christ as its savior figure and one that has Simon Magus as its savior figure.

The school that had Simon Magus as its savior figure, was most likely much more popular and more credible.
We know this from the reports of Justin Martyr, that described Simonianism as a large sect.

Justin Martyr did not attend any of these schools, because he tried to find one that did not take payment,
after trying other philosophical schools.
One day, he met an old man while walking by the shore, which taught him about Jesus for free.

With other words, Justin Martyr was probably poorly educated and an outsider compared to contemporary authors.
Justin did not become a doctor and hence by the standards at the time, some kind of loser.
This probably bothered him deeply the rest of his life.

This means, since much of church history is based on the works of Justin Martyr,
that we have few clues to how Early Christians practiced medicine at the time.
In fact, this knowledge was lost to a such degree that,
when Christianity became the official religion in the Roman empire,
it simply copied Galen's works and used it as the textbooks of medicine,
despite that Galen was a pagan philosopher.
This never bothered Christians, but as soon as somebody else wanted to practice that knowledge outside the church,
they were killed.

As a result, Early Christianity was essentially lost as a practice of medicine,
which was continued as a Dogmatic school of medicine where the practice was pagan,
but the doctrines were Christian.

This development caused many problems: Practice of medicine and doctrines were inseparable in the ancient world.
The fact that the practice of medicine contradicted the doctrines,
made Christianity simply a protector of an economic monopoly on healthcare.
It did not matter much what people believed, only whether they tried to compete.
To hide this inconsistency, the church made teachings as obscure as possible,
by enforcing Latin in Sunday lectures, which the peasants could not understand.
Furthermore, most people were prevented from getting a higher education,
which was the only way to access the textbooks on medicine.

Economically, this is the most successful scam in all of human history.
More wealth was stolen from the poor by the rich as a result of this scheme,
than any other scheme for a scam that has been successful, in any other part of the world or in any other time period.

This is why the idea is very unpopular, that there is a larger competing school of medicine in Rome around the mid 2nd century, worshiping Simon Magus.
It raises questions about Christianity as whole.
Questions that many people would not like answered.

Basically, the controversy here is that the expectations people had from religion,
changed when Christianity became the official religion in the Roman empire.
To claim monopoly on healthcare, is essential robbing people of their freedom of intellect.

Practice of medicine in the ancient world is closely connected with philosophy and history of thought within all ancient cultural traditions.
The loss of freedom of independent practice of medicine, was more or less a ban on culture.
All other cultures, than the Christian culture, was seen as inferior and oppressed by the elite.

Christianity absorbed practice of medicine wherever it went and persecuted the people who taught the Christians new knowledge.
One can think about it a similar way today, how Disney sues people who use fairy tales that Disney copied and claimed copyright on.

This way, the fact that practice of medicine was pagan based, did not slow down the spread of Christianity.

That is why there are many savior figures around in the ancient world, who become replaced with Jesus Christ over time.

The only way to get closer to some historical Jesus, is to peel away this mechanism of corporate abuse around practice of medicine.
However, since this development probably happened after mid 2nd century, plus that we have very little evidence

preventing us from gaining insights into the 1st century.

The best we can do today, is to make multiple hypotheses and explore all of them.

### Was the historical Jesus a doctor?

It is possible that the historical Jesus was a doctor who became famous for his charity,
probably by treating poor patients for free or cheaply, while taking higher payment from rich patients.

This is a typical practice around the world among doctors.
Many doctors are spending significant resources and time to save people.
There is no reason to believe that such doctors did not exist in the ancient world.

Sometimes, a doctor might provoke a person with authority and get killed as a result.

For example, Galen himself moved to another city out of fear of getting killed,
not because he did something wrong, but because, as Galen claims,
out of jealousy by other doctors who threatened him.

When an important person died, it was a custom to celebrate them using a translation fable.
This involves an empty tomb, a witness meeting the person while traveling and an ascension toward heaven.

This is one of the most boring and straight forward explanations of the Jesus Christ savior figure.
Somebody names their school after that person and the rest is just history.

The other part of the myth, could come from a such school making their savior figure a bit more interesting,
to attract more students. Like I said before, a such development would be relatively normal and boring.
While not exactly the average person's life, it might not be interesting enough to compete among schools.

This could even be a basis of this version that contributed to the origin of the literary Jesus Christ character,
without it needing to explain the whole development.
The popular Jesus Christ character was mostly spreading in the late 2nd century by people,
which probably had few connections or poor education, relative to official people from the school of medicine using that precise character.

### Was the historical Jesus the Joshua that succeeded Moses?

In the myth about Moses, who never existed historically, Joshua takes over the leadership after Moses dies.

The name "Jesus" is actually the same name as "Joshua".
We just use different spellings because it makes it easier to distinguish these characters in the bible from each other.

Many people where named Joshua around Jesus' time, often based on that character and relatives named after him.

In some Catacomb paintings, which are the earliest surviving samples of Christian art,
Jesus is depicted side by side with Moses, which signifies that Jesus is a successor of Moses.

There is a possibility that Early Christians thought about Jesus as Yoshua reborn.
This is also a point in a dialogue in all 3 synoptic gospels,
where Jesus is asked whether he is Elijah or one of the prophets (Matthew 16:14, Mark 8:28, and Luke 9:19).

Interestingly, Joshua does not come up as an alternative here, but Peter says that he believes Jesus is the Messiah.
The Messiah for Jews is a figure like Joshua, a human, that will liberate Israel from its oppressors.
However, Messiah as Son of God is a Christian concept.
Here, in Mark, there is no mentioning of Son of God in this context, which means it might refer to a Joshua-like figure.
Jesus warns his disciples of telling anyone about it,
which is kind of obvious if he is thought of as a reborn Joshua,
because it would alarm the authorities.
The idea is that people should wait until the right moment, before they pick up their swords.

In the increasing tensions between Jews and Romans leading up to the Bar Kokhba revolt,
the Joshua figure might have gotten divinized and hence later gospels like Matthew says Son of God in this context.

In the debate of Historicity vs Mythicism of Jesus, there is a debate point around whether Jews were prophesizing about a suffering Messiah.
Here, it is irrelevant, because the main point of Jesus being resurrected, is because he will soon come back and liberate Israel.
Resurrection in itself is not uncommon at the time in contemporary stories, neither was painful deaths or death by execution.
The argument is that suffering and resurrection is not that a big deal, but the emphasis is that Jesus will come back soon, as a Joshua figure.

Another thing that is important in this context, is that Moses and prophets were worshiped as savior figures by Early Christians.
One obvious example, is Melchizedek in the Book of Hebrew.
The author argues that Melchizedek is the same figure as Jesus Christ.

If The Ouroboros Hypothesis is correct, then the resurrection of Jesus in the original Mark has two meanings for Joshua:

- Resurrection "back in time" to Galilee, restarting the story and recruiting more disciples/soldiers
- A literary play on translation fables, where the reader might become the witness, as it is not included in the story

These two meanings engage the reader, which might have been intentional, to convince people to join the cause.

We know that Revelation was written this way, to make Jews rebel against Romans and has been used through history to incite wars.

To try understand this from a modern mindset, one can think about the stories of Santa Claus.
Santa Claus is probably based on a historical character, Nicholas of Myra,
but people put origin stories of Santa Claus in different centuries.
Often a century in the past.

To Jews, savior figures could return, e.g. Elijah, so Joshua appearing in the history of the 1st century,
seen from the perspective of 2nd century Early Christians, is not that strange.
Just because the 1st century historical Jesus might never existed,
does not mean that Jesus Christ is not built on some historical Jesus.
It can simply be Joshua, a Messianic warrior that people thought would come back and liberate Israel.

This is not as straight forward as the historical Jesus as a doctor,
but Jesus as Joshua is a possibility that is worth taking into consideration.
Joshua would be an interesting savior figure for young students joining a medicine school.

You can think of it as an added layer on the story about Jesus Christ that goes around and heal people.
These healings, might have been visions about progress in medicine, used by some school for advertisement to new students.
Now, there is no war in this story, but the specific interpretation of Jesus as a Jewish Messiah or Joshua,
allows the expectation that a war will happen soon.
So, this original Mark would serve both purposes, perhaps precisely because those who incite war,
are using an already popular character as a basis for their propaganda.

The spread of a such story, could have been the motivation of Marcion of Sinope's canon,
because he wants to explicitly avoid the situation where somebody uses his text to incite war against the Roman empire.

As a result, Jesus Christ might have been based on both a doctor and Joshua, but with the layer of Joshua added later.

### Was the historical Jesus a rabbi?

This question is about what it means to be a rabbi in the 1st century or whether somebody might call Jesus with that title out of respect.

A rabbi usually means a preacher that is authorative on teaching the Jewish Law.

Basically, it means that the historical Jesus would, most likely, have to be part of some sect to be given authority.

Since Jesus Christ criticizes some of the sects, it is more likely, if he was a rabbi, that he belonged to a sect that he does not criticize.

One option is the Essenes, but we have very little knowledge of them.

For example, the sects we refer to as the Qumran communities, are given that name by the archeological site of the Dead Sea Scrolls.
We do not know whether the Essenes were located in this area.

Jesus Christ's clothing, linen, matches the description of clothing we have from the Dead Sea Scrolls.

In addition, the gospels describe the period from when Jesus moves to Capernaum until his death,
as a period where Jesus Christ is preaching.
There seems to be a some kind of start in Capernaum, which might be thought of as Jesus starting his practice as a rabbi.

A common requirement to be a rabbi was to be married, but this might vary between sects.
It is also possible that to appeal to new students in medicine schools, that Jesus was unmarried in myth,
but having a lover instead, e.g. Mary of Magdala.
In the Gospel of John, Jesus meets a woman by a well, which was something men at the time did to become engaged.
The woman by the well could have been Mary of Magdala, having previous experience of sexual relationships,
similar to Helen of Tyre in Simonianism, which was a sex slave that Simon Magus purchased and took as wife.
This requires the later text to be altered, but a such alteration is not unreasonable.
There is no introduction of Mary of Magdala as character in John, plus that Zoe is mentioned next to Logos in the beginning.
Finally, in John, Mary of Magdala is the first person who meets Jesus after he has resurrected.

All these things suggests that the Gospel of John might be about a love story between Jesus and Mary of Magdala.
This is just yet another genre among Early Christian texts, that spans multiple genres.
The reason these two are lovers, could be because they were historically married to each other,
but the author unmarried them to make the story more interesting for young people, so the boy gets the girl in the end.

Mary of Magdala could originally have been just "Mary" and "of Magdala" was added to compete with "Helen of Tyre" in Simonianism.
Tyre is a city by the Mediterranean sea, at an extreme geographical point, to the west.
This could be a literary device to make the character more sexual, as the shape of an erected penis.
Magdala is also located at an extreme geographical point, at the west of the shore of the Sea of Galilee.
This could be a literary device to make the character more sexual, as the penetrated vagina.
The movement from East to West is the Sun's path across the sky, with might have been used as a metaphor for intercourse.
In Egyptian mythology, the sky and the earth are thought of as Nut and Geb, making out.
It was common to use bodily actions of deities as a basis to explain hot or wet phenomena in nature.
For example, some Early Christians, according to Irenaeus, thought that Sophia cried the ocean into existence.

So, the idea that the historical Jesus was a rabbi,
might not have been that strange,
even if Jesus was married.
The most likely reason Jesus was not described as married later,
is because people wanted their stories to be more interesting for young people.

If Jesus was not married, perhaps because he was a rabbi in a sect that forbade marriage,
then the Mary of Magdala character could have been invented by some bored Early Christians.

It is possible that the historical Jesus was rabbi, by being in some sect and given authority to preach Jewish laws,
but he might have combined this with other roles, such as being a doctor.

### Was the historical Jesus a human trafficker who used slaves to produce antidotes for snake venoms?

As scandalous this position sounds and easy to debunk, there are several sources that link Jesus to human trafficking and snakes.
We just do not know whether these sources are about some historical Jesus or the literary Jesus.

The Roman empire was based on slavery.

This approach to the topic of a historical Jesus, should be of no surprise to anyone that reads some history.
However, here, there are lots of problems and controversies.

The idea that the historical Jesus could not have been involved with human trafficking,
is based on a Christian bias, that Jesus can not be imperfect in any way.
There is also the idea that Jesus lived in poverty, which makes it difficult to imagine how Jesus could have slaves.

However, there is some ambiguity about this among the texts we have from Early Christians.

In Acts of Thomas, Jesus sells his twin brother Thomas as a slave to a rich man in India.
This is the only record we have of Jesus creating a written document: A slave contract.

Most Early Christians were not opposed to slavery in general.
Paul, at least, was not, who according the Epistle to Philemon, sent back a runaway slave to the owner.
This letter is regarded among most biblical scholars as authentic.
It might not have been written by Paul, but it is believed to be the same person who wrote the seven oldest letters from Paul in the New Testament.

If Paul sent a slave back to the owner, then by definition, Paul was involved in human trafficking.
Can the same the said about Jesus?
Yes, if Jesus sold Thomas as a slave to India, then regardless of the interpretation of a such possible historical event,
Jesus was involved in human trafficking.

There is also a controversy around the Greek word "leistes" in Mark 14:48, that can mean a pirate that is involved in human trafficking.

Human trafficking in the Roman empire happens in two ways:

1. Through official channels
2. Through piracy

If somebody did human trafficking through piracy, then they would get crucified by Roman law.

Most Early Christians, with some few exceptions (Second Treatise of the Great Seth), believed that Jesus was crucified.

If Jesus was crucified and with no further context, then it is not unreasonable to consider that Jesus was crucified for piracy.

Human trafficking was very profitable, so naturally the Romans wanted a monopoly on this trade.
This is one of the reasons they give a such harsh penalty for piracy.
One aspect of this is that they believe it is immoral when somebody else does it to them,
like when Julius Caesar was kidnapped by pirates,
but not immoral when they themselves do it to others.

A part of the plot in Mark is that Jesus is falsely accused of a crime.
This means, if Jesus was a human trafficker operating through official channels,
then he might get betrayed by being accused of being a pirate ("leistes").
Alternatively, Jesus might not have been a human trafficker, but still accused of being a pirate.

Therefore, there is no concrete evidence that the historial Jesus would not be involved with human trafficking,
or being falsely accused of doing it.
However, if the historical Jesus was legally trading slaves, then he would likely not be crucified for it,
unless somebody betrayed him in return of making false accusations.

Now, the remaining part is whether the historical Jesus used slaves to produce antidotes for snake venoms.

Snake bites were feared everywhere in the ancient world, because without an antidote, you could risk death.
For most people, who lived much closer on nature than most people do today, having antidotes ready was very important.

From our modern understanding of medicine, it is dangerous, but possible, to build up immunity against snake venom.
This immunity happens by the immune system producing antibodies that disables snake venom when it enters the blood.

The most efficient way of extracting antibodies to be used in some antidote, would be by using blood.
Antibodies are primarily produced in response to infections or toxins and are found in higher concentrations in blood serum and plasma.

To produce an effective antidote against snake venoms, it would be, more likely, by using blood, instead of other body fluids like semen or vaginal blood:

- Semen is primarily composed of sperm and seminal fluid, which does not contain significant levels of antibodies.
This would make it an ineffective medium for the purpose.
- Vaginal blood primarily comes from the menstrual cycle or childbirth,
and it consists mostly of uterine lining material, red blood cells, and other biological fluids.

Semen or vaginal blood might be used as ingredients in some antidote in attempt to make it more effective, based on religious beliefs.
It could also be something ancient people had more experience with than we have in modern science,
but this is speculation, as it might be difficult to distinguish symbolic significance from biological properties.

There is no solid evidence that slaves were used to produce antidotes for snake venoms.
It is possible, because we do not know how all the ingredients for antidotes, that people write about, were produced.
However, if slaves were used to provide body fluids for antidotes against snake venoms,
and this involves semen of vaginal fluids, then it would likely be a compound of blood and other body fluids.

There is no source that claims Jesus was using blood from any other person.
On the contrary, all the sources say that when Jesus asks somebody to drink something, he says "this is my blood".
However, this can also be interpreted as "my blood" as in "my property", if Jesus took the blood from slaves he owned.
Still, this collides, if the disciples were Jesus' slaves and he gives them a drink containing their own blood,
unless it is meant in the sense "my family", or the belief that exchanging blood makes one family by oath.
Maybe there was blood both from Jesus and his disciples in a drink? Who knows?

There is also another aspect, that using slaves for antidotes could have been done outside the context of religious symbolism and rituals,
but the religous symbolism and rituals might have been partially based on this practice,
without necessarily doing all the steps involved in the process.
This is another layer of complexity that is difficult to interpret for us today, since we are not part of ancient culture.

### Was the historical Jesus a humble carpenter?

In Mark 6:3, Jesus is called a carpenter.

Here, calling Jesus a carpenter might have been a way to talk down on him, not as the proper title Jesus had.

One reason Jesus might have been thought of as a carpenter, is because he was thought of as a son of a carpenter.

From the perspective of advertisement for a school of medicine, this makes sense,
when you think about new students that come from families which have built up enough wealth, through hard work, to get their children an education.

In the same way, perhaps many of these families did not have the opportunity to send all their children to school,
but maybe they sent one or two, which they could afford.
It must have been quite an experience for those kids to arrive in a city, perhaps their first time,
with buildings and temples and wonders, like toilets and baths.
They would probably feel intimidated and search for a school where they felt welcome.

When one sibling gets an education and another does not get the opportunity,
this could frequently have been a ground for jealousy and make the relationship between siblings uncomfortable.

This section in Mark is probably to address these emotions,
because teachers who spend a lot of time with their students usually understand how they feel and how to make them feel better.
From experience, they might have figured out that there are few ways to make the situation better,
because you can not control how other people behave, only how you tackle a situation yourself.
That is why the author recommends traveling to another city, to put some distance between themselves and their family.

In Mark 3:21, there is another section where the family tries to get Jesus home.
This is a contrast between how the family feels and what Jesus feels.
The family wants the student home, although there might be some jealousy, because they might get some education through him,
plus that they benefit in social status by having somebody educated among them.
However, the student feels their home place has become too small, after seeing the world offers much more to be explored.
The student does not want to get stuck and later be humiliated by being compared with ordinary crafts.

It might seem strange today that this takes place in what people consider holy scripture,
but in this literary tradition, it is a sign of mental health and elite culture to confront emotions directly.
With other words, people are wrestling with problems that might have no solution.

One can see this in the example where Paul delivers back a slave that ran away from his master.
Paul knows that it is wrong to deny help to a person who begs him for freedom,
but he is trapped inside a system which whole economy is based on slavery.
If Paul does not deliver back the slave, then Roman authorities will come after him.

It is wrong to think about Paul being persecuted because he is behaving ethically by our standards today.
Paul complains over being persecuted, because that is how the author is processing these difficult emotions.
The end result is usually the same anyway, which is to give in to authority.

In the case of Jesus, he is acting in his own authority, when he decides to move away from his family.
The family wants him home, but they do not have the authority to force him.
When they challenge his authority, Jesus stands up for himself and asserts that his status will be too low in his home place.
If you read it as a mystical saying, then it is not how it was intended,
because the expression of honest emotion behind the words, that might sometimes sound a little cowardly,
is how people write this stuff to rationalize their own actions.

The author does not write to confirm an idealized ethics,
but to confront a problem directly, even there is no solution.

This kind of reasoning is typical for those who take higher education,
so these sections do not count in favor of the historical Jesus being a carpenter.
On the contrary, they point in the direction of a doctor or rabbi instead.

### Was the historical Jesus a magician?

A magician is somebody who can perform tricks that give people the impression of something supernatural happening.

It is not clear to a what degree Jesus is a magician.

The reason this is complex, is because some people might be professional magicians,
while others might do it to entertain people where they go or as part of advertising for their business.

Since Jesus Christ is used as a savior figure for schools of medicine,
it is possible for him in some ways to be more like a professional magician in stories,
compared to the teachers in these schools.
This is because, the entire point is to make new students interested in the education being offered.
Unlike a professional magician, Jesus Christ does not take paid for it in the stories,
because it does not add to the effect of the story as advertisement.
That is why Jesus Christ can both behave more like a professional magician,
but also not do it for the money at the same time.

While is true that there were professional magicians,
a school usually would need some more substantial and useful knowledge to teach its students.
If the savior figure they use is based on some historical Jesus,
then the most likely cause that a such person could motivate somebody to start a school,
would be that the person has some more substantial profession than simply being a magician.

That said, as there were people with the titles "Magus" or "Christ" who attracted attention in the public,
there could be use of these same titles for savior figures to make the schools appear more interesting.

One could make a similar argument for the title "Messiah", but this might be risky and attract negative attention.
A such title would probably be used by somebody who wants to incite war with propaganda.

### Was the historical Jesus a philosopher?

Some scholars argue that the stories about Jesus might have come from one person, or combining multiple historical people,
who, similar to Diogenes or Aesop, had interesting conversations with other people or using parables to make their point.

This option builds more on the modern idea of a philosopher, than philosopher in the sense of people who become doctors.

One problem with this option, is that interesting conversations or parables might be produced by many kinds of people,
not just certain characters that are closer to the concept of modern philosophy e.g. cynic philosophers.

The historical Jesus here can be difficult to distinguish from folklore in general.
There might be multiple historical people, plus story tellers who make up their own contributions.
In the extreme case, one might say that a historical Jesus would be the set of authors that wrote texts about this character.

Also, by extension, if there is a story about Jesus where he says something that might be according to some philosophical tradition,
then one could argue that the philosophers in that tradition make up the historical Jesus as group.

There are two extremes here:

1. The historical Jesus is one person that did philosophy similar to our modern concepts of philosophy
2. The historical Jesus is a character where many people who did philosophy used to express their own views

If the historical Jesus was a doctor, then almost by definition, he would also be a philosopher.
Philosophy was part the mental training to become a doctor.

Many Early Christians thought about themselves and what they were doing with the texts, as arguing philosophically.

It is possible that the historical Jesus was somebody who did philosophy similar to our modern concepts of philosophy,

From the perspective that there was a person who said things that people wrote down and copied,
this is not that much different from the authors who write something creatively about what a person might have said.
The only difference is whether you give the person in the story more credit, or the author of the story.

This boils down to whether you think it is important whether some historical Jesus said something,
or whether you are thinking about all people who contributed to the philosophy of literary Jesus.

These two views of a historical Jesus are in conflict in one sense, but they also might be mixed in another sense.

Do you see the problem?

When it comes to expressing pure ideas, it is no longer easy to credit these ideas to some historical person,
because different people use different judgements of how to credit such ideas.

For example, let us say there was a historical Jesus that said some ideas in public that were written down.
If that historical Jesus heard these ideas somewhere and merely repeated them,
then does this make this historical Jesus a philosopher?

If Mary of Magdala told Jesus what to say, then does not this make Mary of Magdala the philosopher instead?

If Moses did not exist, but Aaron did and said some things Moses supposedly told Aaron to say,
then does not this make the Moses character based on the historical Aaron?

If a historical Jesus said something, but everybody misquoted him,
then an author a century later accidentally claims Jesus said what he actually said, but the author was lying,
then is that quote based on an actual historical Jesus?

It is easy to tell whether some ideas come from some philosopher,
but it is not easy to tell whether some literary person is based on some historical philosopher.
Unless, there is something about that person that is characteristic, or the ideas are unique.
At least, not in the modern sense of a philosopher.

There is another layer of similar complexity,
where an author writes a story with a character based on some historical person,
but despite that this happens, officially, the historical person is discredited from the character.
Perhaps because it is an embarrassing story, or something.
Yet, readers of the story might know who the character is based on.

### Was the historical Jesus an eremite?

Most scholars focus on versions of historical Jesus that might be more controversial or engaging for people to read about.

An eremite is one of the most boring things to write about in the ancient world.

The possibility that the historical Jesus was an eremite, is definitely boring, but it is something that should be considered seriously.

Ancient people were frequently visiting oracles and eremites located in isolated places to seek advice from the deities.

Now, you might think that it would be more efficient to seek advice from somebody who know them and have the social connections to provide help.

Modern people tend to forget one thing: Just like modern people, ancient people wanted to figure out their own problems, when they could.

Seeking help from others is not easy, because people feel that they owe them something in return later on.
This can produce a feeling of being lesser worth for society or under the control of somebody who are stronger.

The solution to this dilemma in the ancient world, is that when people believe they can figure it out themselves,
or they have no others to talk to about their problems, they use an oracle or an eremite as a surrogate for the time they need to think about it.
With other words, the main mechanism is to use the tough journey to process things and when an oracle or eremite give them some mysterious answer,
people interpret the answer as something they have been wanted to hear all the time, but were afraid to admit it to themselves.

There is evidence that Early Christians kept records of oracle sayings.
Just because some oracle said something to some person, does not mean that people thought of it as irrelevant in other context.
So, by collecting oracle sayings, people could contemplate and meditate on their own problems.
This serves as a basis for spending time on processing information in general,
that give people the opportunity to handle their issues with less pressure or stress.

An eremite can also function like a cynic philosopher.
However, in such cases, it is always difficult to trace back ideas,
because people frequently made up stories that they attributed to some philosopher or character.

Some eremites might also take religious wovs, for example to not speak or to live on top of a pillar.
This does not mean that the eremite was not visited less.
On the contrary, the more impractical it was to ask eremites for advice, they more people would talk about them.

There is a possibility that the historical Jesus did not travel much or said anything of significance.
People might use such characters to create their own stories.
The medium people use to retell such stories could have influenced how people perceive a such character.

Despite that Paul claims met Jesus,
the only letters we have from somebody who might witness something about the historical Jesus,
that might date back partially to the 1st century,
he did not actually meet any Jesus who was alive.

The Jesus that Paul claims he met, is in his view a resurrected Jesus.

This means, Paul could have been influenced through other people,
or he could participate in a translation fable as a fake witness,
a way to honor the dead Jesus.

In any case, Paul does not give us much details about the life of Jesus.
This means, Jesus might have been an eremite who was arrested and crucified,
perhaps as a way to punish people who frequently visited this eremite,
for rebelling, or not paying taxes, or similar.

The loss of an oracle or an eremite that served an important social role for a group of people,
might have resulted in these people continuing their relationship to this person after the moment of death.
To express support for this eremite, they might declare themselves as disciples or apostles of that person.

Now, this might sound stupid, but it make sense when you think about it from this perspective:
People in general like to solve their own problems and frequently attributed sayings and made up stories about some character.
By making an eremite spiritual, they also were guaranteed to not lose that relationship later on.
With other words, making an eremite spiritual can be the result of learning from an authoratative oppression tactic.

### Was the historical Jesus an esoteric figure like Honi HaMe'agel?

Honi HaMe'agel, also known as Honi the Circler, was an esoteric figure that performed miracles.
What characterizes such figures is that their role or relationship to the divine is unclear and ambiguous.
These stories consist of two parts, one where miracle is performed and second when people react to it.
The main point of these stories is to build in the natural skepticism that listeners have,
since a pure claim of the miracle itself is less believable.

Culturally, the use of ambiguity can draw more attention toward figures that are lesser known.
People like to figure out things, try new things, and they also like to put togoether the puzzle themselves.
One of the reasons Jesus became popular as savior figure, is precisely because these stories do not give you a complete picture of who Jesus was.
Later, when some figure becomes culturally dominant, it is often linked to established authority, who makes bold claims with confidence.
Only authority has the "correct interpretation" of the myth and opposing views might result in persecution,
since challenging accepted ideas is closely related to forming rebellions.
Esoteric figures incites curiosity and enforces superstitious beliefs in the super-natural in general,
which attracts outsiders and people with less power in society.

### Was the historical Jesus a cult leader?

Cult leaders are often charismatic and tell people what they want to hear, so they can be manipulated socially.
One common cult tactic is called "love-bombing" where potential recruits are overwhelmed with attention and love they seek.
At the same time as the cult gives recruits what they want, they also isolate them from the rest of society.
This makes the recruit emotionally and psychologically dependent on the cult, which decreases the chance they will leave.

In Early Christian writings, there is often a demand that people being recruited need to give up their own property.
As idyllic this might sound to somebody in favor of a collective ownership of property today,
it is a bait-and-switch tactic done by cults to make the recruit economically dependent on staying loyal to their leader.

A cult leader would say that they come to give rewards to the people who they try to recruit,
but also at the same time warn of conflict with friends and family.

All these cult attributes are present in the texts about Jesus that Early Christians wrote.

### Was the historical Jesus an apocalyptic preacher?

An apocalyptic preacher is somebody who warns about the end of an era and beginning of a new era.
Apocalyptic preaching is often associated with cults, because certainty in doom is emotionally easier to process psychologically than the uncertainty of the future.

Here, it is very clear that a recurring theme in Early Christian writings about Jesus,
is the prophecy of doom. This is one of the reasons that most scholars today believe that Jesus was an apocalyptic preacher.
