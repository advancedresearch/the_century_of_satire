# Mary of Magdala - The Reincarnated Goddess

To reconstruct 2nd century cultural literature context of Roman satire in Early Christian texts,
it is useful to form a hypothesis on what kind of role Mary of Magdala has from the perspective of mystery religion.

One thing that makes Early Christianity truly unique as a mystery religion in the history of the world,
who drove conversion of the first Christians as initiated to this new mystery cult (perhaps starting in late 1st century to early 2nd century),
was a theological achievement of attempting to unite Eastern mythology with Greek, Roman and Egyptian mythology,
to make Jesus or Simon Magus seem like the true heir of Eastern religions, in addition to these other religions.

This theological achievement was to use a reincarnated goddess as a representation of the Eastern religions.
Like the central concept of escaping the eternal cycle of reincarnation in Hinduism and Buddhism to achieve enlightenment or nirvana,
this reincarnated goddess achieves salvation by (as you might guess) the savior figure of the mystery religion in Early Christianity.

### 1. Do Early Christians even care about identity metaphysics - yes, no - yes!

Identity metaphysics is a hard philosophical problem of how to create a theory about identity over time.
The problem has baffled philosophers for over two millenia and is still pretty much unsolved, even today.

The ship of Theseus, the mythical king of Athens who rescued the children of Athens from King Minos,
was a specific ship taken on a pilgrimage each year to Delos, the alleged island that Theseus escaped to.
In order to keep this tradition, people had to repair the ship by replacing one broken part with a new one.
As the years go by, ancient philosophers asked the simple question: Is this ship still the same ship that Theseus escaped on?

Perhaps this question was asked to distract from talking about the actual history of Theseus vs Minos,
which is about the collapse of the Minoan civilization (the Keftiu people living on Crete and other islands)
resulting in centuries of slave trade, severe hunger where people engaged in cannibalism and hiding in the fog-covered mountains.

Anyway, the answer to whether a ship is still the same ship after replacing every part, might be answered yes or no, depending on perspective.
In one sense it is the same ship by its form, which resembles the original ship.
In another sense the ship is not made out of the same materials that were used in the original ship.

When people eat food, the food gets ingested and broken down chemically and used to build new molecules of amino acids,
which are the building blocks of the body. While people in the ancient world did not know about amino acids,
they were smart enough to figure out that the body was made up of divisible smaller parts that changed over time.
Today we know that after 7 years, not a single atom is the same, demonstrating the relevance of Theseus' paradox for understanding human lives.

In the extreme case, when people in the ancient world imagined the afterlife,
they were concerned about whether you existed as a spirit in Hades or actually got a materialistic body.
Some people sought an escape from their materialistic constraints and looked forward to floating around as a spirit.
However, other people were more anxious and worried that floating around as a spirit would get boring over time.

This leads to the idea that what preserves identity metaphysics over time,
as if it was a magical solution to Theseus' paradox,
was neither the spirit nor the materialistic body, but a third thing: The soul.

The soul could attach to a body or spirit, but it could also be detached.
One way that people thought like this was because if you can change every part and yet still preserve the same identity,
then this proves that there is some part of mortal beings that is in fact immortal, at least in some sense.
However, since spirits overlapped in language usage with the invisible bodies of deities, archeons or angels,
they needed a third term for the soul to make it special for human beings.
The soul brought more tools to the table to play with in theology.

Early Christians use Logos or Nous as the Greek philosophical concept as the original source of salvation.
Logos or Nous is supposed to interact with Zoe or Ennoia, which represents the soul that needs salvation.
With other words, salvation does not mean just to belong to a particular group of people that worship the same savior figure,
but something that transcends the materialistic and ordinary divine...
that just happens to be only accessible at the moment by belonging to a particular group of people that worship that same savior figure.

The actual joke is that when Early Christianity evolves into Late Christianity,
they change the theology for salvation, just like with the ship of Theseus,
while keeping the fact that... by all means it is no surprise when considering mystery religions as business models...
the salvation happens to only be accessible at the moment by belonging to a particular group of people that worship the same savior figure.
Duh.

Did Early Christians care about historicity? No. To any extent they wrote about any ordinary event with ordinary people,
we do not have the names of the particular people involved, despite that Early Christians wrote thousands of texts
across at least three different languages spread across multiple nations spanning over a century of time.
There are so many people involved in this bizarre activity,
that seemingly made no attempt to enforce a rule to not write about ordinary people,
yet also did not do it despite given plenty of opportunities,
that we can conclude that they simply were not interested in historicity, at least in a modern sense the way we think of history.

So, in what sense do Early Christians care about anything, at all?
Surely, they wrote a lot, so they must have cared about something to write this body of literature, yes?

The answer to this question is controversial no matter how you look at it.
So far, the evidence points to Early Christians caring about theology and philosophy,
or they care about drugs and medicine, or more likely:
A mixture of theology and philosophy with drugs and medicine.

A clear sign of drugs being involved, is that ordinary people do not usually develop such strange concepts of identity metaphysics.

The basic problem of identity metaphysics is that whatever people care about in the future surrounding their own agency,
they need some kind of mental model of what is the correct identity metaphysics,
because this determines how they assign values in the mental landscape of the imagined future.

With other words: Caring itself as an activity is closely related to identity metaphysics.

Any self-consistent philosophical perspective has to at least interact with this issue at some point.
So, when a whole new mystery religion seemingly disregard this basic idea, it might seem very surprising from a scientific view.
One would expect that if they care so deeply about theology and philosophy that they forget to mention anyone doing anything out of historical interest,
then they would at least engage with the very core of the problem of identity metaphysics, at least to some degree.

However, this is not what Early Christians do. They never bring this up. Not even once.

The answer is that mystery religions - as the name suggests - see the basic answer to everything as a mystery.
People in these religions believe firmly that that the only way to truly understand it... is by not understanding it.

Sigh... do you see where this is going?

Think about Socrates' "The only thing I know is that I know nothing."
Or, about the paradoxes of koans in Zen Buddhism,
that practitioners of meditation used to reach new stages of enlightenment, such as "What is the sound of one hand clapping?".

Like Wittgenstein claiming to have solved all philosophical problems by constructing a paradox of communicating facts,
or viewing use of language as a language game,
the mystery religions play similar moves to try to make their own theology unsolvable,
and by this achievement patting themselves on the back as a reward,
followed by making highly detailed specific doctrines about rituals and afterlife that are designed to bring in profit,
instead of giving people enlightenment.
This type of scam is as old as religion itself.

So, instead of claiming Logos or Nous as the way, which people later might perceive as Christianity,
the Early Christians combine Logos or Nous with Zoe or Ennoia,
while not caring the least that these are two different and inconsistent views of identity metaphysics in the afterlife,
which produced a so complex theology that no single person could grasp it and they had to dumb it down in Late Christianity.

I am pretty sure you do not really get it yet, so I am going to spell it out in big letters, for your appreciation:

THE EARLY CHRISTIANS BELIEVED YOU HAD TO BE SAVED TO GET AN AFTERLIFE,
BUT ALSO, THEY BELIEVED YOU HAD TO BE SAVED FROM ETERNAL REINCARNATION.

### 2. Why mystery religions aim their message at Eastern culture

The thing is that established mystery religions at the time of Early Christianity,
were so popular that people traveled the entire way from India to get initiated.

This means, in order to get a foot inside the door for a new mystery religion,
you have to come up with a way to address the Indian culture,
not because most of the target audience is from that culture,
but because people will compare the new doctrine with established mystery religions
and disregard it if it does not address the same questions that established mystery religions already address.

It is kind of, when people sell new cars, they have to come with functionality that might be of little interest to the person buying the car,
but since the competitor sells car with this feature, the customer will decline the offer unless the feature is added.
The salesman know that the person is not interested in the feature,
but also, they do not get sales when the person notices something expected is missing.
In the end of the day, what matters for the salesman is to sell cars, not teach people to evaluate cars in a rational way.

So, what Early Christians do, is pretty clever:
They know that people expect something along the lines of Indian culture to make their religion comparable,
but they do not want to go overboard with this idea.
After all, their main audience is from a specific culture that does not easily accept these ideas.
Yet, the stuff needs to be in there somewhere, to make the religion mystical and exotic enough for people to care.
What they do, is to apply it for just one character.
This means that there is a doctrine that holds for all people in the religion except for this one character,
that follows its own set of rules that applies to no one else.

More impressively, you are not allowed to live by the rules of that one character.
No, the character is **saved** from that condition.
Yet, it happens that in the sense this one character is saved is also overlapping with enlightenment in Indian culture.
This means, although the goal is fulfilled, normal people are not allowed to go that route,
because the route applies only to one character and not for normal people.
Indian culture is not accepted as a way of life, it is merely tolerated as an exception, in case somebody drops by as a guest.

This mental gymnastics is so impressive that there is some sort of tolerance built into Early Christianity,
that does not explicitly condemn a particular way of living that can seem exotic or make people interested,
but also not approve of that way of living. It is not sin, but it is also a kind of moderate sin-but-not-sin?

Now, the problem is that you can not explicitly say "an Indian woman" because this is so rare at the time that people at the would not believe it.
Instead, people use a character that is from "somewhere else", but not too far to be unbelievable, combined with a reincarnated goddess to link to Eastern religions.

- In Simonianism, people link this role to prostitution as the tolerated but not accepted way of living and Tyre as the somewhere-else-but-not-too-far place.
- In Christianity, people link this role to demon possession as the tolerated but not accepted way of living and Magdala as the somewhere-else-but-not-too-far place.

### 3. Simonianism and Helen of Tyre - parallels to Jesus and Mary of Magdala

In Simonianism, Helen is a sex slave that lives in Tyre, which is saved by Simon Magus, the savior figure.
She is a reincarnated goddess, Ennoia, that was trapped into the materialistic body during the moment of creation and reborn.
One of the lives she lived was as Helen of Troy, the cause of the war of Troy and the red line in the popular epic by Homer.

Simonianism is considered part of Early Christianity, although it has a different savior figure than Jesus.
This is a complex relationship, because what became Late Christianity coevolved alongside Simonanism,
borrowing ideas from each other. Simon Magus even appears in Acts of The Apostles, attempting to buy the Holy Spirit.

This was probably because Simonianism during the middle 2nd century, around the time when Acts of The Apostles is written,
is more widespread and more wealthy than the competing Christian sect, that produces this text as a sign of jealousy.
The author of Acts knows that what Simonianism offers compared to their own message is like choosing between loaf or bread,
so the best way to get more followers is to down-talk the other sect by turning a strength into a weakness.
Otherwise, it is unlikely that Simon Magus would be mentioned at all.

Mary of Magdala plays a similar role to Helen of Tyre.
Magdala and Tyre are both cities placed at geographical extremes in the local region.
Tyre is the most west point against the sea, while Magdala is the most west point along a lake.
The point is to make the story more exciting and when people get bored or unimpressed by this nearby city,
they change their mind and say that no, Mary of Magdala was actually Greek (e.g. Mariamne in The Gospel of Philip).
They do not say she is from India, because that would not be believable,
but by making her Greek, she could be from anywhere, including Alexandria or Rome, or even far east close to India.
So, by letting people use their imagination, they do not specify the location because they get more followers that way.
Besides, the importance is to keep the spotlight on Jesus.

When Jesus is born in Bethlehem, he is visited by three wise men from the East that bring him gifts.
These three wise men serve a similar role to Mary of Magdala in the sense they refer to Eastern religions.
However, they do not have a continued presence in the story, which makes them less useful than Mary of Magdala as a literary device.
The three wise men appear solely in the Gospel of Matthew and were interpreted as Magi, like in Simon Magus.
With this element in the story, the author of Matthew can downplay Mary of Magdala's role and refer to her as a Jewish woman.
The reason the author of Matthew does not say the three Magi came from India, is again that it would be unbelievable,
but East is vague enough to also include India and this satisfied the people who considered getting initiated.

Mary of Magdala were associated with prostitution, like Helen of Tyre, possibly because the parallels between the two characters.
Yet, unlike Helen of Tyre, Mary of Magdala is not explicitly associated with it,
which makes it possible to interpret as coming from a higher class in society.
Mary of Magdala is also mentioned alongside Joanna, the wife of Chuza,
seemingly in an attempt to tell that she could not have come from a lower class,
since Joanna occupied one of the highest statuses a woman could have in society at the time.
No one would think that Joanna would meddle with people involved in prostitution.

The language used in the gospels uses ambiguity deliberately, such that people can think Mary of Magdala was a prostitute,
if they want to, or that she was from a higher class, educated or raised knowing Greek, or even from a place far to the East.
We find the same ambiguous language used about Jesus, when he is rumored to eat with sinners,
without confirming or denying that the rumor is true, or who the sinners were and what they did.
Like, when Jesus picks two disciples that are helping their father with fishing,
yet they have a nickname that might be used for bandits, but without saying explicitly that they were fishermen,
because the nickname does not fit that role, but also without saying explicitly they were bandits.

However, the clearest sign that Mary of Magdala played a similar role to the reincarnated goddess in Simonianism,
is that in the beginning of Gospel of John, the word Zoe is used, which might refer to Tree of Life (Zoe = Life, Zoe's tree in Greek).
We know from Ireaneaus' Against Heresies that Zoe was the consort of Logos before the creation of the universe.
There is no more important female character than Mary of Magdala in John, which could mean she was meant to represent Zoe
and was the first person to see Jesus after the resurrection.
Zoe gets saved by Logos from eternal reincarnation and is used as a metaphor for spiritual salvation.

Zoe in this sense could mean Eve, the mother of life, that could have been a goddess in stories before she was created from Adam's rib in Genesis.
We now know that the version of Septuagint that Early Christians used were Theodotion's translation from mid 2nd century,
so the first Early Christians might not have used Genesis the way they did in Late Christianity.
It is also unclear whether the earlier Septuagint was the original text that was translated into Hebrew
and this was backtranslated into Greek or a possible combination of the two that influenced later developments in theology.
Eve in the earlier Septuagint is related to a chant to worship Dionysus,
so it is possible that from the very start, from the time the library of Alexandria was built and forward,
Zoe was connected to Eve in a way that is compatible with the idea of the reincarnated goddess in Early Christianity.

This changes the priority of Mary of Magdala from being a historical figure,
to playing an important theological role in an effort being made over centuries to appeal to Eastern religions,
to not fall behind in the race among the established mystery religions.

### 4. Saturninus of Antioch - breaking away from Simonianism: Say hello to Jesus, a new savior figure

The earliest somewhat reliable external record of anyone practicing or teaching a doctrine with Jesus as a savior figure,
is not from any late 1st century or early 2nd century historians or contemporary letters among Roman officials,
nor from church fathers, but from what was in modern times called a Gnostic tradition.
It is Saturninus of Antioch around 100 AD, that breaks away from Simonianism to teach about Jesus.

This is the first time we get the name of any particular Early Christian in the context as a practioner of the religion,
not in the context of persecution or historicity but as in a reference to the religion itself,
although Saturninus is not an ordinary poor Christian, from which we have not even a single record during the entire 2nd century.

This link to Simoninanism reoccurs later the same century, when Marcion of Sinope, joins a Simonian school in Rome under Cerdo.
Marcion of Sinope is arguably the most influential person in the evolution of Early Christianity.
Why would Marcion join a Simonian school, when claiming to be a follower of Paul?

This is a baffling historical relationship that shows that, according to the little evidence we have,
Jesus as a savior figure more or less evolves out of Simonianism, that worships Simon Magus as their savior.

However, when thinking about the parallels between Mary of Magdala with Helen of Tyre, this relationship makes more sense.
If this reincarnated goddess appeared in both these sects, then it means there is more reason to believe that the role of this character
is tied to new attempts to update mystery religions to become more relevant for a new age.
Whether one belives that Jesus was copied from Simon Magus or not, is less relevant,
because it is the side characters that really tell what kind of literature context these stories were used.

In some sense, understanding Mary of Magdala as a literary device might help to unlock the historical context of Early Christian texts.
There is a way to interpret the two Marys, Mary Jesus' mother and Mary of Magdala, as both representing earlier established traditions,
but from different aspects where Mary Jesus' mother follows the tradition back to Medea and Isis cults,
while Mary of Magdala follows the tradition back to Eastern religion or some-place-else-unspecified-but-possibly-Greek,
that appealed to new people in regions where Early Christianity spreads.

### 5. Josephus as potential origin of this theological achievement - which started as satire

More than an amazing historical coincidence (so there is reason to be skeptical),
we might have an idea of where this theological achievement originated from,
of bringing up a reincarnated goddess being saved.

Josephus' Antiquities of the Jews tells a story about some Atomus (which means small or indivisible),
was hired as a Magi to convince Drusilla to divorce her husband and marry Felix.
In Latin translations the name Simon was sometimes used, plus that the name Paul carries the same meaning (small).
This might explain why Marcion of Sinope could join a Simonian school and yet claim to be a follower of Paul.

Marcion claims to be a follower of Paul, but he also puts Paul beneath Jesus as a savior.
This might be his way of appealing to both Simonians and other sects that used Jesus instead.
To convince his followers, Marcion presents letters by Paul, which never before has been presented to anyone or found someplace else.
We know that these letters were heavily redacted, added to and even forged new letters in Paul's name.
While some Early Christians later claimed the authenticity of these letters,
we have no source from any person that knew Paul, nor from any of Jesus' disciples,
so the historicity of these letters might be taken with a grain of salt.

Now, with Paul's letters in mind, it is easy to explain Acts of The Apostles as a later text that responds to Marcion of Sinope.
In Acts we get a revision of history that splits Simon Magus and Paul into two different characters.
Also, Acts is a sequel of The Gospel of Luke, in which Peter is a disciple by Jesus that previous was named Simon.
Acts uses Josephus heavily and refers to the marriage between Drusilla and Felix using Paul visiting them in person.
This links the reference in Josephus to the whole interraction between different Early Christian sects,
which makes Josephus a more likely origin.

Josephus was a Jew, so Greek might not have been his native language.
The use of Atomus could have been interpreted by people, e.g. some Roman poet,
as either an intentional move that passed censorship, or as a hilarious unintential coincidence,
that the section about Drusilla and Felix is read like excellent Roman satire where Atomus,
meaning small, refers to Felix' private parts.
With other words, one can read this section as a dick joke,
which also in the historical context is very controversial for this very reason:

**Drusilla and Felix' only child dies in the eruption of Mount Vesuvius, a rare event that was interpreted as judgement by the deities.**

Now, keeping this in mind, the warning that Paul gives to Drusilla and Felix in Acts, gives a special ominous interpretation.

This means that Early Christianity texts could have evolved as a literary genre out of Roman satire,
where a historical event was brought to the attention of some Roman poets, of a controversial marriage with catastrophic ending.
The entire root of Simonianism and other sects, including Simon Magus and Jesus,
could have been people reading these stories written by poets and thinking they were actual historical events,
following by their own effort to bring something new into these stories in the competition between mystery religions.

It is not uncommon that people at the time write stories before some important historical event,
to make it seem as if the prophecies become true.
Jesus in this role serves as a holy man, a Magi, that is crucified, bringing a tragic ending.
In protest, Yahweh leaves the temple of Jerusalem,
which opens up the city for invasion by the Roman army.
However, Jesus resurrects, in the style of other Hellenistic translation fables and takes the throne from Yahweh.

Contrary to later expectations, Yahweh at the time is not an all-knowing deity.
This is a deity that is more humanized and can make mistakes.
So, in Early Christianity, many sects believe that Yahweh needs replacement and Jesus is the heir to the throne.
Jesus in some sense tricks Yahweh and takes the throne when turning back from the dead,
but also, some sects have serious doubts of whether it is a good idea to go all the way,
so they make Jesus say that the way to Yahweh only goes through Jesus.

It is a complex business to sort out all these theological arguments and disputes,
but it is in this context that Mary of Magdala appears as a side character,
which might be or not be a reincarnated goddess, depending on the version people got initiated into.

While Mary, Jesus' mother, has a more clear role as the superior female character just below Jesus in status,
because this character goes back to the tradition of Medea and Isis cults,
the role of Mary of Magdala is more ambiguous, because she represents the visitor from East, or the person to be initiated.
This character is more troublesome for later Christians, that do not know where to put her in the social hierarchy.
After a while, Christianity is made the official religion of the Roman empire, so it becomes rarer that people a new to these beliefs.
This development puts Mary of Magdala even more on the edge as a character,
because as somebody who is meeting Jesus for the first time and potentially from some place else,
where is she from? Where does she belong?
So, in classical Christian style, Mary of Magdala is swept under the rug and the historical traces back to Simonianism is covered up.
All we find today is bread crumbs of what these texts might have originally been.
